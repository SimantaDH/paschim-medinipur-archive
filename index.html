From Oral Tradition to Digital Archive

Audio-visual storytelling & cultural memory in Paschim Medinipur — Gajan ritual performance and the Patachitra scroll-painters of Naya, Pingla Block, West Bengal.

Research & field documentation — Disha Pramanik & Simanta Nandi, MA Digital Humanities & Social Sciences, IIT (ISM) Dhanbad

keep scrolling

Abstract

Heritage as something practiced, not something framed

Digital technologies are reshaping how Paschim Medinipur's living traditions are remembered. Instead of monument-centred heritage, this project stays with the district's performative practices — Gajan's rites of endurance and devotion, and the scroll-and-song storytelling of Patachitra in and around Naya village, Pingla Block.

These traditions are woven into agrarian rhythms, devotional practice and collective memory, and they are now meeting migration, commercialisation, tourism and digital circulation all at once. Rather than freezing them into records, this study proposes digital humanities methods — community-run websites, GIS mapping of ritual and sacred space, and audio-visual oral history — that keep gesture, voice, song and seasonal variation intact.

Four kinds of repositories anchor the model: ethnographic documentation, metadata-cataloguing, community-curated archives, and spatial (GIS) mapping. Local practitioners, performers and patua artists are treated throughout as co-archivists — not subjects being recorded, but people directing how they are represented.

The aim is not preservation as freezing, but continuity: a participatory approach where technological innovation and local agency, oral tradition and cultural memory sit at the centre of the archival process, together.

Two Living Traditions

Gajan & Patachitra, side by side

Both traditions are agrarian, devotional, and intergenerational — one performed on the body, the other on cloth. Switch between them below.

In Naya village, all 176 households — known collectively by the surname Chitrakar, "artist" — paint patachitra scrolls and sing the patar gaan, the song of the painting, as they unfurl each panel (pata khelano). It is audio-visual storytelling that predates the moving image: mythology, epic, and now child marriage, AIDS awareness and the pandemic, painted with pigments made from turmeric, soot, conch shell and river clay.

The women patuas of Naya — among them Ruksana, Swarna and Radha Chitrakar — have moved the tradition onto a global stage over the past two decades, while artists like Khadu and Bahadur Chitrakar keep the mythological repertoire that collectors and scholars still prize most.

Gajan is a pre-harvest, hot-season festival of the Rarh region devoted mainly to Shiva, observed across villages of Paschim Medinipur including Rahatpur, Anandapur and Ramjibanpur. Devotees undertake fasting, hook-swinging, body piercing and fire-walking as acts of endurance and faith, closing with the Charak ritual and a communal feast.

Fieldwork for this project gathered audio and video testimony from priests, lifelong participants, diaspora visitors and younger observers — voices ranging from a 70-year-old woman devotee married into the ritual fifty years ago, to a 24-year-old student encountering it as a "cultural critic."

Patar Gaan — Song of the Painting

Three scrolls, three voices

Every patachitra panel is sung as it is unrolled — pata khelano. These excerpts, recorded in the field in July 2024, show how one painted scroll can hold ecology, devotion and social critique at once.

Shobai Mile Koro Gaach Ropon — on planting trees together+
In Bengali

Shobai mile koro gaach ropon O Jonogon! Shobai mile koro gaach ropon Gaach lagaile porey manusher upokaar kore…

In translation

Let us all plant trees together O People! Let us plant trees together Planting trees benefits people later…

Performed by Shiuli Chitrakar, Naya Pingla — a call to ecological responsibility, read alongside the UN Sustainable Development Goals.

Saanjher Belae Jamunar Jol — on Radha and Krishna at dusk+
In Bengali

Saanjher belae jamunar jol ante bole key Na jaaner kon kalar shone mon mojeche Kodom tolae uthei abar bongshi bajae ke…

In translation

Who calls for Jamuna water at dusk? Do you not know who the dark one is who holds my heart? Who plays the flute again under the kadam tree?…

Performed by Ruksana Chitrakar — dusk as the threshold between the ordinary and the divine, in the Bhakti register of Radha–Krishna devotion.

Dariya Maacher Biye — the wedding of the river fish+
In Bengali

Dariya maacher biye ditey cholo go rangeela Dui kaatla bole ami biyer palki noye jabolo rangeela…

In translation

O Rangeela, let us go to marry off the river fish Two katla fish say, we will carry the wedding palanquin…

Performed by Sabera Chitrakar — local biodiversity and Bengali wedding custom, with the boal fish read as a folk allegory for social hierarchy.

Ethnographic Method

An interview built to be handed back

The questionnaire below was designed so respondents could reflect on ownership as much as memory — who should hold these recordings, and who gets to say what belongs in them.

A · Participant information+
  • Village / locality, occupation, role in Gajan (performer, organiser, devotee, musician, priest, artisan)
B · History & cultural memory+
  • What stories, myths or local histories are tied to Gajan here?
  • Have the traditions changed — and how?
C · Performance & participation+
  • Who takes part — men, women, children, elders?
  • How is the knowledge passed down a generation?
D · Community & heritage+
  • What should be preserved, and why does it matter to you?
  • Is younger participation declining?
E · Digital documentation & archives+
  • Who should manage these archives — government, researchers, or the community itself?
  • Should the community control access to its own records?
F · Community-curated heritage+
  • Would you contribute your own songs, stories or footage to an archive?
  • What support would help preserve this for the next generation?
G · Final reflections+
  • What should future generations know that hasn't been asked yet?

Theory in Practice

Why "co-archivist," not "informant"

Four scholarly threads shape the model this project borrows from — brought, deliberately, into a rural Hindu–Muslim agrarian setting that this literature rarely reaches.

The archive and the repertoire

Diana Taylor's distinction between the written archive and the embodied, performed repertoire is the central problem this project sits inside: how do you archive a gesture, a swing of the hook, a sung line, without freezing it?

Community-centred collecting

Michelle Caswell's principles for community archives — broad participation, shared stewardship, many voices, archiving as activism, ongoing reflexivity — read here as a working checklist, not a slogan.

The participatory archive

Isto Huvila's term for decentralised, user-directed curation names exactly what happens when a patua chooses what gets filmed, and a priest decides what stays undocumented.

Authorized Heritage Discourse

Laurajane Smith's critique of who gets to decide what counts as "heritage" is the reason this project starts from performance, not from monuments or listings.

Sustainability & Ethics

Building a project that can also end well

Digital humanities work is often described as facing a coming "digital dark age" — the rate at which we produce digital material has outpaced our ability to keep it usable. The University of Victoria's Endings Project frames this as three open questions this archive has to answer honestly.

01 · Conclusion

How should a DH project end?

Not every archive needs to keep growing forever. Planning a stopping point — and what "finished" looks like — is part of the design, not an afterthought.

02 · Preservation

Can it stay usable and dynamic?

Static files outlast custom code. Wherever this project can trade a clever feature for a durable format, it should.

03 · Custody

Who holds it, and on what terms?

Distributed archiving raises its own questions of access and intellectual property — which is precisely why community authority over the material, not just institutional storage, has to be built in from the start.

Attribution as protocol

When POT Maya installed bilingual signage asking visitors to credit each artist and village before posting photos online, it wrote an attribution ethic into the festival itself. This project treats that signage as a working model, not a footnote.

Software with the ethics built in

Where possible this project favours platforms like Mukurtu, designed for granular, culturally-specific access protocols, over a generic content management system — and looks to the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance as a baseline, alongside Dublin Core and CIDOC-CRM for shared metadata.

The Endings Project's Five Pillars

University of Victoria's Principles for Digital Longevity (v2.2.1) split any digital humanities project into five parts. This site follows all five deliberately — including, in a small way, by being built as the plain static HTML/CSS/JS file you're reading right now.

01 · Data
Open formats only

Stored in formats built for the long haul, not boutique ones — this project's own field data (interview structure, pigment table, song texts) is kept as plain text and markup, not a proprietary database.

02 · Documentation
Rights made explicit

Data models and licensing should be spelled out in a static document that travels with the data — see the licence panel just below.

03 · Processing
Validate relentlessly

Any processing code should validate its own inputs and outputs rather than fail silently — good practice whether the "code" is a build script or a field-data spreadsheet.

04 · Products
No fragile stack

"No boutique technologies... no dependence on external libraries." This page is one HTML file with inline CSS/JS — no build step, no framework, no dependency that can quietly disappear.

05 · Release
Versioned, citable

Every release should carry a build date and version number, and make clear how to cite it. See the version stamp and citation tool below.

FROM ORAL TRADITION TO DIGITAL ARCHIVE
Paschim Medinipur, West Bengal · v1.1, built 2026-07-08

A community-facing prototype archive built as a digital humanities research artifact. Field data, translations and images belong to the communities of Naya, Rahatpur, Anandapur and Ramjibanpur, and to the researchers who recorded them with consent. Sustainability approach adapted from the Endings Project, University of Victoria. Built as a single static file for hosting on GitHub Pages.
